Consequences

If every job on this list were eventually eliminated, what would follow? The consequences are not only economic — they are social, psychological, and moral. Below are some of the most discussed, drawing on the writings of John Maynard Keynes and Joseph Schumpeter.

The economic problem, solved

Keynes predicted that within a hundred years the “economic problem” — the struggle for subsistence — would be solved, with living standards four to eight times higher. With scarcity gone, the central challenge becomes how to use the freedom from “pressing economic cares” and the leisure that “science and compound interest will have won.” Work, which has always given life structure and purpose, would no longer be compulsory.

The psychological shock

Keynes warned of a “general nervous breakdown”: people bred for countless generations to strive for subsistence may not know how to “live wisely and agreeably and well” without the spur of economic necessity. He pointed to the “wives of the well-to-do classes” who, deprived of traditional tasks, could neither enjoy leisure nor find anything more amusing. The transition from a work-centered identity to a leisure-centered one is, he thought, a fearful problem for the ordinary person.

Creative destruction

Schumpeter saw the same process as “creative destruction”: the perennial gale that “incessantly revolutionizes the economic structure from within, incessantly destroying the old one, incessantly creating a new one.” Lost jobs, ruined companies, and vanishing industries are inherent to growth. Societies that try to reap the gain without the pain “find themselves enduring the pain but not the gain.”

Redistribution and meaning

When labour is no longer the source of income, the link between work and survival breaks. Questions of distribution — who receives the abundance produced by machines — become political rather than economic. New social customs, moral codes, and institutions would be needed to share the “bread thin on the butter,” to spread the remaining work widely, and to cultivate “the art of life itself” rather than the accumulation of wealth.

A slower transition

Both writers cautioned that this change comes gradually, not as a catastrophe. Keynes suggested three-hour shifts or a fifteen-hour week as a way to defer the problem; the “old Adam” is strong enough that most people will still want some work, some duty, some routine. The pace depends on controlling population, avoiding war, trusting science, and the rate of capital accumulation.

Sources: John Maynard Keynes, Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren (1930), full text; Joseph A. Schumpeter, Capitalism, Socialism, and Democracy (1942), summary.
